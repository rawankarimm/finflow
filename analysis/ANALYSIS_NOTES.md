# Analysis Notes

## 1. Time Series: Daily Transaction Volume, Fraud Count, and Mean Amount
The time-series plots are useful for distinguishing between ordinary transactional activity and abnormal behavior that may indicate fraud. If transaction volume rises sharply while fraud counts remain stable, the increase may reflect legitimate economic expansion or seasonality rather than misconduct. By contrast, spikes in fraud count that coincide with elevated transaction values can be interpreted as periods of heightened risk, consistent with the idea that fraud tends to cluster during moments of stress, uncertainty, or rapid change in cash flows. The mean-amount series also matters because large payments often carry more reputational and financial consequence, making them more attractive targets for manipulation. In finance, this kind of pattern is often read as a signal that the system’s risk environment has shifted, rather than as a simple volume artifact.

## 2. Amount Distribution: TRANSFER vs CASH_OUT
The comparison of log-transformed amounts for TRANSFER and CASH_OUT transactions helps reveal whether the two categories follow different statistical regimes. If the distributions are similarly shaped but shifted, this suggests that the underlying economic behavior differs mainly in scale rather than in structure. A strong right-skew in both groups is consistent with the common financial observation that extreme payment sizes are rare but economically meaningful, which is why log transformations are often used in risk modeling. In fraud detection, this matters because thresholds based on raw amounts can be distorted by a few very large observations, while log-scale analysis provides a more stable benchmark for outlier detection. The fitted normal curves offer a simple way to judge whether the empirical data behave approximately as expected under a log-normal process.

## 3. Balance Drain Distribution
The balance-drain distribution is especially informative because it captures whether account balances are being depleted in a way that looks mechanically consistent or suspiciously extreme. A large share of observations with absolute balance drain greater than one suggests data-quality issues or structural inconsistencies in the transaction records, which can undermine any downstream fraud model. From a finance perspective, this is important because balance-based indicators are often used to test whether cash movements are economically plausible. If the observed distribution is concentrated around zero but has long tails, the tails may represent rare but material liquidity shocks or possible manipulation, and that distinction is critical for designing rules that do not overflag normal customer behavior.

## 4. Interpretation for Fraud Detection Rules
These charts collectively support a risk-based view of fraud detection rather than a purely rule-based one. Transaction volume, amount size, and balance dynamics should be interpreted jointly because fraud is rarely captured by a single variable alone. In financial theory, abnormal behavior is often best detected when one looks for departures from expected patterns in multiple related dimensions simultaneously. For that reason, the plots can guide threshold-setting by showing where the data are concentrated, where the tails begin, and which variables are most likely to separate ordinary activity from suspicious activity. The practical implication is that detection rules should be calibrated not only by statistical extremes, but also by the economic plausibility of the observed transaction behavior.

## 5. Macro-Enrichment Findings
The macro-enrichment exercise tested two plausible economic hypotheses using monthly transaction aggregates linked to FRED series. For the unemployment and cash-out case, the Pearson correlation was positive at 0.3481, while the Spearman correlation was slightly negative at -0.0972 and the OLS regression explained about 12.1% of the variation in cash-out volume. That pattern suggests a weak but potentially meaningful relationship: when unemployment rises, households may draw down liquidity and increase cash withdrawals as a form of consumption smoothing. However, the evidence is not strong enough to treat this as a clear causal effect without further controls or a lag structure, because the relationship could also reflect other macroeconomic forces operating at the same time.

For the USD/EUR exchange-rate and transfer-amount case, both the Pearson and Spearman correlations were small and negative, and the regression fit was very weak with an R-squared of only 0.0165. This implies that cross-border transfer volumes do not appear to move strongly with the exchange rate in this sample. The observed relationship may reflect a spurious correlation driven by shared macro trends such as global risk appetite, inflation, or shifts in remittance demand, rather than a direct causal effect of FX movements on transfer intensity. In macroeconomic terms, exchange-rate changes can matter for remittance behavior through transaction costs and incentives, but the present evidence is too weak to support a strong causal claim.
